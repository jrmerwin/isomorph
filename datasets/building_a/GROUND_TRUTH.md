# Building simulation — ground truth and benchmark

A semi-real multivariate time series: a small office building, three zones
(A, B, C), each with its own heater and ventilation unit, 60 days at 1-minute
resolution. Generated by `simulate_building.py` (seed 7, deterministic); default
symbolization in `symbolize.py`; Markov baselines in `baselines.py`.

Files
- `building_1min.csv.gz` — 86,400 rows × 30 columns: timestamp, minute, day,
  weekday, T_out, RH_out, setpoint, and per zone occ, T, CO2, RH, heater,
  vent, window; plus vibration (unrelated) and power_kW.
- `building_events.csv` — 12,253 events (≈204/day) from the default
  symbolization: minute, timestamp, day, zone, variable, event, value.
- `sim_params.json` — every constant.
- `baselines.txt` — the numbers below.
- `fig_sample_day.png` — day 36.

## Mechanisms (what a complete-system model should recover)

Each zone runs two coupled loops with delays, and the loops are identical
across zones (zones are interchangeable entities).

**Thermal loop** (minutes to tens of minutes)
- Heater turns ON after T has been below setpoint − 1 for 2 min; OFF after T
  has been above setpoint + 1 for 2 min. Cycle length ≈ 1–2 h at night, faster
  by day.
- T rises at 0.15 °C/min with the heater on; falls toward outdoor at 0.0035/min
  of the difference (≈ 0.05–0.1 °C/min on a cold night).
- Setpoint 21 °C 07:00–19:00 on weekdays, 17 °C otherwise. The 07:00 setpoint
  step (zone = ALL event) triggers heater ON in every zone within minutes — a
  building-wide cause with a zone-level effect.
- Solar gain peaks at 13:00; warm afternoons (zone T > 23.5 °C with people
  present) make occupants open a window with prob 0.01/min; windows close
  after 10–40 min or when T < 20. A window cools the zone fast → heater ON
  10–30 min later. Rare (≈ 1–2 window events per zone per week), long lag.

**Air-quality loop** (tens of minutes)
- People add 6 ppm/min each; vent ON after CO2 > 900 for 3 min; OFF after
  CO2 < 700 for 3 min; vent exchange 0.06/min of the excess over 420 ppm.
  With ≥ 5 people the vent cannot keep up and stays on for hours (see zone A
  and C on day 36); with 2–3 people it cycles every ~40 min.
- Ventilation also cools the zone (0.015/min of the outdoor difference) and
  dries it (pulls RH toward outdoor). So: **arrive → CO2 up900 → vent on →
  T drifts down → heater on** is a genuine five-step cascade spanning both
  loops, with lags of ~10 min (arrive→900), 3 min (→vent), 15–40 min (→T
  below band), 2 min (→heater). It occurs on most weekday mornings in zones
  with ≥ 3 people.
- RH: people add moisture, vent and heater remove it; RH crossings are a
  slow, noisy echo of the vent loop.

**Occupancy** (the exogenous driver): weekday arrivals ~08:30 ± 25 min,
lunch 12:20 for ~70% of people, departures ~17:30 ± 35 min, a 1-hour meeting
(+2–4 people) on half the days at 10:00/14:00/15:00; weekends sparse. Zone B
has one fewer person on average.

**Outdoor**: diurnal 14 ± 8 °C with slowly varying weather (AR(1) per day);
RH_out anti-phase. T_out crosses 10 °C most days and 20 °C on warm days.

## Regimes and complications (the parts standard models struggle with)

1. **Zone C comes online at day 30** (no data before: NaN). A model that
   binds by entity can transfer the A/B loops to C on day 30; a model that
   keys on variable names must relearn.
2. **Fault, zone B, days 40–49**: the damper sticks; vent effectiveness ×0.1.
   CO2 climbs to 2000–4000 ppm, vent stays on for hours, the air-quality
   loop stops cycling, and the thermal loop sees extra cooling. Same
   vocabulary, different dynamics: a regime change in one zone only.
3. **Weekly cycle** (weekday/weekend) and **daily cycle** (setpoint, solar,
   outdoor). Cycle-preserving nulls are needed for any temporal claim.
4. **Chatty sensor**: `vibration` is an AR(1) noise channel with ~1,300
   threshold-crossing events over 60 days, unrelated to anything. In the
   default symbolization it is 11% of all events.
5. **Dropouts**: T_B is missing in random 5–20 min gaps (≈1% of minutes).

## Baselines (predict the event type of each event, given its zone)

Train days 0–29, test days 30–59. Interpolated-backoff Markov models.
Bits per event, lower is better (uniform over 30 types = 4.91).

| model | all | zone B, fault days | zone C, first 5 days | non-heater events |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| unigram | 4.27 | 3.58 | 3.65 | 5.05 |
| global context, order 1 | 3.97 | 3.17 | 3.62 | 4.81 |
| global context, order 2 | 4.21 | 3.44 | 3.79 | 5.16 |
| global context, order 3 | 4.37 | 3.64 | 3.81 | 5.40 |
| **zone context, order 1** | 2.64 | 2.58 | 2.34 | 3.30 |
| **zone context, order 2** | **2.49** | 2.61 | 2.27 | **3.14** |
| zone context, order 3 | 2.60 | 2.84 | 2.24 | 3.28 |
| zone context, order 2 + lag class | 2.52 | **2.49** | 2.24 | 3.21 |
| zone + building-wide context, order 1 | 2.98 | 2.73 | 2.56 | 3.84 |
| zone + building-wide context, order 2 | 2.90 | 2.80 | 2.55 | 3.82 |

What the table says about where the room is:

- **Entity binding is worth 1.5 bits per event.** A Markov model on the
  interleaved building stream barely beats the unigram and gets worse with
  longer context; the same model restricted to each zone's own history drops
  to 2.49. The zone-context models already pool across zones (tokens carry no
  zone), so they also get cold-start transfer for free (zone C at 2.27).
- **Longer plain context does not help** (order 3 overfits on 30 days); the
  remaining structure is not low-order sequential.
- **Building-wide events hurt a Markov model** (2.90 vs 2.49): adding the
  setpoint, outdoor and vibration events to each zone's context pushes the
  real causes out of the window with chatter. A model that can *select*
  which context matters (the setpoint step, not the vibration crossings)
  and bind it at the right lag is what would recover this.
- **The fault regime is where lag helps** (2.49 vs 2.61) and where order 3
  hurts most (2.84): the dynamics changed, the vocabulary didn't.
- **Non-heater events are at 3.1 bits** against a 4.9-bit ceiling: the
  heater on/off pair (29% of events) is nearly deterministic and inflates
  every model's average. Score non-heater events separately.

## What "winning" means on this dataset

A method is worth pursuing only if, scored prequentially on days 30–59 with
training on days 0–29, it beats the zone-context order-2 Markov model on
non-heater events (3.14 bits) by a margin that survives the same test on a
second seed, AND at least one of:
- it handles the building-wide triggers without being poisoned by the
  vibration channel (beats 2.49 overall while using ALL-zone context);
- it adapts to the zone B fault faster than a model retrained from scratch
  (bits on days 40–41 vs days 45–49);
- it recovers the five-step cascade and the setpoint→heater link as explicit
  structures, which the Markov model cannot represent as objects at all.

Reproduce: `python3 simulate_building.py && python3 symbolize.py && python3 baselines.py`
(≈ 10 s). Change `SEED` for a second instance.
